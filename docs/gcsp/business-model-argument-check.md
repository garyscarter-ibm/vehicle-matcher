# Business model argument check

31 October 2026. Working analysis using the build-an-argument skill; accompanies the [business model exploration](business-model-exploration.md). Evidence references below point to that note's source list. Proposed business effects remain hypotheses.

**Subsequent clarification:** Russell's model begins with lifestyle/taste attributes and actively shapes employee choice and future used supply. Employee model preferences and residual outcomes are not fixed inputs. The main exploration has been revised accordingly. The tests below still apply to feasibility, fair allocation and full-cycle economics, but they must assess alternative supply/demand responses rather than only a static list of acceptable models. ## Overall argument

**Claim:** A reciprocal exchange of advance preferences and a more dependable allocation process could improve employee and scheme outcomes within a commercially acceptable fleet cycle.

**Proof in hand:** The research records constrained choices, manual ordering work, unallocated cars, a gap between requests and Production confirmation, and tension with residual value. **Still needed:** current scale of each problem, employee acceptance at real terms, discretionary supply and lifecycle economics.

**Warrant:** Early information and knowledge of acceptable alternatives can reduce avoidable mismatches, but useful mutual gains depend on actual alternatives, acceptable cost and credible rules.

**Objections:** Hard supply shortages may leave little allocation gain; expressed preferences can be strategic or change; forecast employee demand may conflict with used demand.

**Conclusion:** A defensible conditional proposition to test, not proof of savings or better residuals.

**Reasoning risks:** Treating the rush as proof of employee satisfaction; assuming every cause is a prediction failure; assuming three personas capture all financial interests. Address by separating mechanisms and including reward, Production and remarketing perspectives. Honesty and reliability are baseline expectations, not the differentiating claim.

## Sub-argument 1: Existing supply can sometimes serve employees better

**Claim:** Allocation across employee needs can sometimes improve outcomes without increasing supply or changing prices.

**Proof in hand:** The two-car example demonstrates a logically possible gain; historical notes support a mismatch concern. **Still needed:** distribution of actual needs and acceptable alternatives, comparison with current results, effects by employee group.

**Warrant:** Giving the scarce option to someone who needs it while offering an equally acceptable alternative to a flexible employee can improve a joint outcome.

**Objections:** Some employees have no acceptable alternatives; stated flexibility may not predict satisfaction; narrow claims can gain strategic advantage.

**Conclusion:** The logical mechanism stands; the BMW opportunity size is unknown. Do not promise universal first choices or claim the example measures real gains.

**Reasoning risks:** Weak analogy if a real trade-off is presented as costless; aggregate scores mistaken for fairness. Test acceptable outcomes and repeated disadvantage, not only total match scores.

## Sub-argument 2: The terms of the exchange determine participation quality

**Claim:** Pricing, commitment and priority rules can determine whether preference disclosure improves planning or instead rewards strategic behaviour.

**Proof in hand:** Current-car history can be constrained; dated guidelines fix prices at order, distinguish points from contributions and limit changes after approval. **Still needed:** authority for dynamic points, employee attitudes to delegation and priority, evidence of how price changes affect demand.

**Warrant:** People have reason to conceal or restrict preferences if honesty raises their price or consistently assigns them a worse option; a nonbinding preference is weaker ordering evidence than acceptance at known terms.

**Objections:** Employees may accept a published demand-responsive price as fair; a stronger priority advantage could increase disclosure and convenience.

**Conclusion:** Pricing and allocation are substantive benefit choices. They need an explicit bargain with employees and the reward owner, rather than being treated as presentation details.

**Reasoning risks:** Assuming personal or dynamic pricing is always unacceptable; assuming a nudge proves a genuine preference. Distinguish policies and validate observed responses.

## Sub-argument 3: Future ordering needs the whole fleet cycle

**Claim:** Better employee demand information improves procurement decisions only when it arrives before ordering and is assessed alongside feasible supply and future disposal economics.

**Proof in hand:** Greg submits requests before final employee choices; Production confirms availability; scheme-viability restrictions and residual concerns are recorded. **Still needed:** requested/confirmed/released/selected quantities, current planning deadlines, residual exposure, volume-sensitive disposal proceeds and actual funding.

**Warrant:** The extra requested vehicle must both serve an employee and have an acceptable total cost at the margin. More popular employee stock need not attract equivalent used-market demand.

**Objections:** Employee and used preferences may align; residual diversification may be less important than production availability; extra employee value may justify an explicit subsidy.

**Conclusion:** The lifecycle test stands. It does not establish the optimal mix, that today's mix is wrong, or that preference-led ordering necessarily damages residuals.

**Reasoning risks:** Confusing retail price with cost, forecast residual with realised proceeds, points with new cash, or entity-level transfer value with group value. Maintain a consistent accounting boundary and avoid counting depreciation twice.

## Consolidated proof gaps

- Real eligible demand at stated prices, dates and specifications, including unmet acceptable needs and access differences.
- Current requested, confirmed, released and accepted mix, with reasons for losses at each step.
- Production discretion and the time at which preferences could still affect orders.
- Current price-setting, allocation and delegation authority.
- Funding, ownership and residual-loss allocation; realised used proceeds and costs by return cohort.
- Evidence that changes improve employee outcomes and workload, rather than just clicks or preference submissions.

These are recorded as [questions for Sandeep](questions-for-sandeep.md); several require Greg, HR/reward or the commercial owner to answer. No off-subject market numbers were substituted for BMW scheme evidence.

## Consolidated objections

Scarcity can persist after better matching; prices can shift advantage to employees able to top up; manual-route users may lose access; preferences may be strategic; flexible employees may bear repeated compromises; pre-release demand can arrive too late; used-market economics may conflict with employee wishes; attractive vehicle presentation may heighten disappointment when supply is unchanged.

## Structural flags for the next discussion

Do not collapse allocation, procurement and pricing into one claimed improvement. They have different mechanisms, evidence needs and decision owners. The rush has multiple plausible causes with no verified current weighting. The business proposition remains conditional until the scarce resource, allocation bargain and acceptable lifecycle subsidy/risk are understood.
